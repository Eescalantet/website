1. The big idea in 60 seconds

The chapter opens in San Diego. If you drive 16 miles south from downtown to the border, household income falls gradually, from about $85,000 to about $50,000. Then you take one step across the border into Tijuana and income drops to about $20,000. Neighborhoods a few feet apart can't be that different "naturally," so the jump has to come from the line itself.

Regression discontinuity (RDD) uses the same logic. Many programs are handed out by a rule with a cutoff: score 70 or lower and you get a tutor; earn below $X and you get a check. People just barely on either side of that cutoff are almost identical. If their outcomes jump at the cutoff, the program caused the jump.

In the chapter's words, the recipe has three parts:

  1. Account for how the running variable normally relates to the outcome (draw a line on each side).
  2. Focus on the observations close to the cutoff.
  3. Compare the just-barely-treated with the just-barely-untreated. The size of the gap is your estimate.

2. Six words you need

We'll use the tutoring example from Lecture 10 throughout: students take an entrance exam, those who score 70 or lower get a free tutor, and at the end of the year everyone takes an exit exam.

Running variable(also called the "forcing variable") The number that decides who gets treated.Tutoring: the entrance-exam score.
Cutoff(also called the "threshold" or "cutpoint") The value of the running variable where treatment switches on.Tutoring: a score of 70.
BandwidthHow far from the cutoff you're willing to go and still call people "comparable."Only use students who scored 65–75? 50–90?
Sharp vs. fuzzySharp: the cutoff decides treatment completely (0% → 100%). Fuzzy: crossing the cutoff only raises the chance of treatment.Fuzzy: some students at 68 skip the tutor; some at 75 hire one anyway.
KernelA weighting rule. Observations closer to the cutoff count more. The common "triangular" kernel gives full weight at the cutoff and fades to zero at the edge of the bandwidth.A score of 69 counts more than a score of 61.
LATELocal average treatment effect: the effect for people near the cutoff, not for everyone.How much tutoring helps students who score about 70, not students who score 30.

200 new samples, same settings. Each dot is one estimate. A good method is centered on 10 (no bias) and tightly bunched (precise).

  • Try 1: Straight lines, bandwidth ±45 (all the data), then "Repeat 200 times." The dots bunch tightly, but around the wrong number. That's bias: far from 70, the straight line doesn't match the curve.
  • Try 2: Bandwidth ±3. Now the dots center on 10 but spread out a lot. That's noise: there are only a few students near 70.
  • Try 3: Find a middle setting (around ±8 to ±12) where the dots are both centered and fairly tight. Then switch to curved lines at a wide bandwidth and compare.
The core tradeoff (the chapter returns to it several times) A wider bandwidth gives you more data and a more precise estimate, but it brings in people who aren't really comparable, so the estimate is more likely to be biased. A narrower bandwidth is more believable but noisier. There's no single right answer. That's why researchers report results for several bandwidths (Lecture 10's advice: also try double and half your bandwidth).
Don't fix a bad fit with a wild curve If a straight line doesn't fit, it's tempting to use a 4th- or 6th-order polynomial. The chapter warns against that: high-order polynomials swing wildly near the edges of the data, which is exactly where the cutoff is. The book's rule of thumb is to go no higher than quadratic. If you need more flexibility, zoom in with a smaller bandwidth and keep the line simple (this is "local linear regression").

4. Why this counts as causal

Here is the chapter's causal diagram (Figure 20.1), simplified. Z stands for everything we can't measure, such as motivation, family background, and test anxiety.

Tutoring has a back door, because tutoring ← cutoff ← entrance score → exit score, and Z affects the entrance score too. RDD handles this by looking only right at the cutoff. There, a student at 69.5 and a student at 70.5 have essentially the same Z and the same "normal" relationship with the exit score. The only thing that changes is tutoring. So the jump isolates the tutoring → exit score arrow, even though we never measured Z.

Why RDD papers usually skip control variablesIf the cutoff really does assign treatment as good as randomly near 70, there are no back doors left to close, so there's nothing to control for. Adding many controls can even look suspicious, as if you didn't trust your own design. (Controls are more common in fuzzy designs, or to make the estimate more precise.)

5. What has to be true (and how to check it)

Assumption 1: Nothing else jumps at the cutoff

RDD really measures the effect of crossing the line and credits all of it to the treatment. If a score of 70 also got you into a summer camp, you couldn't separate tutoring from camp. The chapter's example: if a score of 95 gets you into both the gifted program and a museum field trip, RDD can't tell the two apart. You can't test this assumption directly. You have to know your setting.

How to check it: placebo tests. Run the same RDD on things tutoring can't affect, such as age, gender, or parents' education. They should not jump at 70. If they do, the two sides aren't comparable. (If you test 20 variables, one or two may "jump" by chance. That's normal.)

Assumption 2: Nobody manipulates the running variable

The design depends on people landing just above or just below the cutoff by luck. It breaks if:

  • People control their own score. The chapter's example: a store discounts tuna if you buy 12+ cans, and you choose how many cans to buy. Here, students who want a free tutor could deliberately underperform to score just below 70.
  • Someone else nudges the score. A teacher regrades a 94 to a 96 to get a student into the gifted program, or a friendly timer shaves two seconds off your mile.
  • The cutoff is set after looking at who it affects. A coach sets the team cutoff at 5:37 right after his son runs 5:35.

How to check it: the density (McCrary) test. Plot a histogram of the running variable. Without manipulation it should be smooth across the cutoff. A pile-up on one side and a hole on the other is a red flag.

Assumption 3 (practical): enough precise data near the cutoff

RDD throws most of the data away, so you need a lot of observations near the cutoff (the lecture calls RDD "greedy"). The running variable also has to be measured precisely. If scores were only reported in bins like "60–69" and "70–79," you couldn't compare a 69 with a 71.

6. Sharp vs. fuzzy RDD

In a sharp RDD everyone below the cutoff is treated and nobody above it is. Real life is often fuzzy: crossing the cutoff only makes treatment more likely. The chapter's example (Battistin et al., 2009) is retirement in Italy. When men become eligible for a pension, the share who are retired jumps by about 30 percentage points, not 100.

If you ran a regular RDD on consumption, you'd see a small jump, because only 30% of people actually switched. The fix is to scale up: divide the jump in the outcome by the jump in the treatment rate. This is instrumental variables (IV), and you'll see it again in the IV lectures, with "being past the cutoff" as the instrument.

Fuzzy RDD effect = jump in the outcome at the cutoff ÷ jump in the share treated at the cutoff

Mini-calculator

When the jump in treatment is 100 points (everyone switches), the fuzzy estimate equals the sharp estimate. The smaller the jump in treatment, the more you have to scale up, and the noisier the estimate gets.

7. What you actually estimate: a LATE

Because RDD only uses people near the cutoff, it tells you the effect for people near the cutoff, a local average treatment effect.

When that's usefulIf the school is thinking about moving the cutoff from 70 to 72, the newly eligible students look just like the ones you studied. The LATE is exactly the number it needs.
When it's a limitationThe chapter's example: relief checks with a $75,000 income cutoff. RDD tells you the effect for people earning about $75,000, but nothing about the effect on people earning $20,000, who probably need the money most.

8. Doing it in R (with AI help)

In this class, AI writes most of the code. Your job is the design and the checking. These are the steps an RDD analysis should include and what to verify at each one:

StepTypical RWhat you check
1. Center the running variablemutate(entrance_centered = entrance_exam - 70)Is the cutoff right, and is treatment on the correct side (≤ 70 here)?
2. Plot it firstrdplot(y, x, c = 70) or a binned scatter with ggplotCan you see a jump with your own eyes?
3. Simple regressionlm(exit_exam ~ entrance_centered + tutoring, data = filter(df, abs(entrance_centered) <= 10))The tutoring coefficient is the gap. Is its sign sensible?
4. Robust estimaterdrobust(y, x, c = 70)What bandwidth did it choose? Does the estimate hold up at double and half that bandwidth?
5. Manipulation testrddensity(x, c = 70), rdplotdensity()Is there a pile-up or a hole at 70?
6. Placebo testsRepeat step 4 with a pre-treatment variable as yNo jump in things tutoring can't affect.
(Fuzzy only)rdrobust(y, x, c = 70, fuzzy = treated)Is there a clear jump in the share treated?
Watch the sign with rdrobustIt assumes treatment is on the right of the cutoff. When treatment is below the cutoff, as with tutoring, the sign of the estimate is flipped: a "−10" means tutoring raised scores by 10. The chapter also shows that rdrobust and a hand-built regression can give quite different answers when they use different bandwidths. A package doesn't make the decisions for you.
Common mistakes
  • Forgetting to center the running variable. The treatment coefficient is then no longer the gap at the cutoff.
  • Using a 5th-order polynomial to "fit better."
  • Reporting only one bandwidth.
  • Claiming the effect applies to everyone (it's a LATE).
  • Skipping the density test and the placebo tests.
  • Clustering standard errors on the running variable. The chapter says this used to be common, but don't do it.
