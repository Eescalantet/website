Friends or Foes?

The insurgent's dilemma: seek legitimacy and risk exposure, or keep secrets and risk isolation.

Chapter I

Why hide, and why be seen?

Insurgents need civilians. Legitimacy, which Weber called the recognized right to rule, brings food, shelter, recruits, intelligence and willing cooperation. Winning legitimacy means telling people things: who you are, what you want, what you have done.

Every disclosure is also a vulnerability. Information about leaders, operations and members can reach the state. So each rebel group faces a trade-off:

RevealGain popular support, resources and collaboration with non-combatants, and lower operating costs. The price is exposure to detection.
ConcealProtect the group from defection and detection. The price is isolation, an image of opacity and deceit, and room for resistance to grow.

The paper's argument is that the binding constraint is the likelihood of splintering. A radical faction that split from a larger movement has more to lose from leaks. Its leaders are easy to identify, and one arrest or defection can bring down the whole group. A coalition of factions can afford to be seen, because losing one member or faction does not sink the movement.

Chapter II

Same war, same enemy, opposite strategies

Peru in the 1980s and 1990s is close to a natural comparison. Two communist insurgencies fought the same state, in the same country, with similar ideological inspiration and transformative goals. One chose secrecy and the other chose legitimacy.

Cells of three overseen by small groups follow the Shining Path's structure as described in the paper; the six-member, evenly split directorate follows the MRTA after its 1986 merger with the MIR–Voz Rebelde.

Secrecy keeps each capture small: an arrested cell member can name only a couple of comrades. But the structure has a single point of failure. In 1992 an elite police unit, the GEIN, captured Abimael Guzmán and his inner circle in Lima after years of surveillance. With its leadership dismantled, the Shining Path rapidly declined.

Openness spreads the risk the other way. No single arrest decapitates a coalition, but every captive or defector can name many people. That is how the MRTA eventually came apart: an amnesty law that offered reduced sentences for cooperation set off a wave of defection and betrayal.

Chapter IV · Exclusive

The Shining Path: a dwarf star

By 1970 Abimael Guzmán had been pushed out of his party, and at least four groups claimed to be Peru's true Communist Party. His Red Faction started with just over a dozen members, led by a philosophy professor and a handful of students at the University of Huamanga in Ayacucho, far from Lima. Carlos Iván Degregori called it a dwarf star: small, but dense enough to have outsized influence.

Secrecy as protection (H1). Recruits had to burn their bridges, often by taking part in punishing their own neighbors and authorities, and they took noms de guerre. One of the top leaders, Elena Iparraguirre, explained:

"We prepared internally, leaving the world, breaking completely with the system, and fully committing to the revolution… Much of our initial strength came from the break between our militants and the world around us, including our families."Elena Iparraguirre, in Zapata (2017, 114)

Secrecy builds hierarchy (H2). Rising through the ranks meant gaining access to concealed information, so decision-making concentrated at the top. "The Party ends and begins with the Central Directorate and its leaders," Guzmán declared. He seldom appeared in public, and at one point the counterinsurgency believed he was dead. Unchecked leaders pushed the riskiest and most violent tasks onto lower-ranking members.

Monitoring without exposure (H3). The party advertised its own secrecy. It claimed "one thousand eyes and one thousand ears," so members watched themselves. Cells of no more than three people reported to groups of five to nine who knew each other and no one else. "It could be your best friend," a villager said. Public sessions of "criticism and self-criticism" disciplined anyone who hesitated.

"I live a double life, teaching by day and organizing by night."Comrade José Vizcardo, in Starn and La Serna (2019, 221)

The Shining Path never tried to explain, justify or even take credit for its attacks. Its leaders also stayed away from the violence they ordered.

Chapter V · Inclusive

The MRTA: revolution as publicity

The Túpac Amaru Revolutionary Movement grew out of a merger of established left-wing parties (the PSR-ML and the MIR–El Militante) whose stated aim was "to forge the broadest possible union with other left-wing parties and organizations." It worked with Chile's Miristas and Uruguay's Tupamaros and had foreign members in its ranks.

Disclosure for legitimacy (H1). MRTA fighters wore uniforms, camped outside populated areas, invoked the Geneva Conventions, and chose targets for symbolic effect: the US embassy, Citibank, Kodak, and seven simultaneous attacks on KFC restaurants. They stole Peru's first coat of arms and its original independence flag to present themselves as heirs to a patriotic tradition. They also redistributed stolen chicken, milk and rice in Lima's shantytowns.

Armed actions that put people at risk

Consensus moderates (H2). MRTA militants had voting rights, and after the 1986 merger the directorate's six seats were split evenly between the MRTA and the MIR. Requiring more members to agree reduced the external costs imposed on any one of them and limited risky violence. It also made decisions slow:

"Because unity within the group is not a function of party hierarchy and party authority, it must be continually reinforced or renegotiated by ensuring that agreement is reached on matter of group policy… suboptimal policies will tend to be adopted to ensure group consensus."Gordon McCormick, report to the US Secretary of Defense (1993, 53)

Openness replaces monitoring (H3). Shared information created common expectations, so the MRTA needed less surveillance. Its top leaders, Víctor Polay Campos and Peter Cárdenas, fought in the field and were both captured in action. When the MRTA took the town of Juanjui, population 12,000, the only people who pursued the insurgents were journalists.

"Reporters snapped pictures of the peasant women dancing the tunche, a local dance, with the bearded and mustachioed guerrillas, who never stopped clutching their guns as they danced the night away."La Serna (2020, 93)

Three hypotheses, two cases

Shining PathA faction that split from the left. It cut recruits off from the outside world, refused alliances and foreign backing, and took its arms and supplies from its victims.
MRTAA merger of parties that shared power with partners, kept channels open to outside organizations, and publicized its operations and motives.

Shining PathSecrecy concentrated decisions at the top, so leaders could impose riskier and more brutal tasks on the rank and file.
MRTAConsensus spread costs across members and moderated violence, at the price of slow, negotiated decisions and a short-lived 1985 truce.
H3The more leaders' and operatives' preferences diverge, the more centralized insurgencies need mechanisms to align them.+
Shining Path"One thousand eyes" surveillance, cells of three, and criticism and self-criticism sessions. Even so, leaders disowned massacres such as Lucanamarca: "it was a mistake that cannot be repeated."
MRTALittle need for monitoring. Leaders fought alongside members, credit was claimed openly, and the reputational costs and benefits of actions were shared.
Chapter VII

Which strategy worked better?

The secretive group mobilized far more people. Its brutality and secrecy gave it tighter control over a large constituency, and recruitment came to rely increasingly on coercion.

Openness won sympathy but not commitment. Many people benefited from the MRTA's actions without paying any cost, and its urban strategy did not resonate in the countryside. When commanders were captured, MRTA fighters in Lima turned to kidnapping for ransom, which cost them more public support. By 1995 most remaining fighters had been captured. In December 1996 the group seized the Japanese ambassador's residence in Lima and took hundreds of officials and diplomats hostage. More than four months later a military raid freed the hostages and ended the MRTA insurgency.

Secrecy bought control and fragility. Openness bought sympathy and exposure.

Chapter VIII

Why it matters now

More than 180 armed conflicts around the world today involve both state and non-state actors. Insurgencies still walk the line between legitimacy and secrecy:

HamasSought legitimacy through governance and public services while planning attacks from public institutions and laundering funds through charities.
TalibanDecentralized decision-making, with governance built on ties to local religious and tribal leaders.
HezbollahKept its members' affiliations secret until attacks on its communication network exposed their identities.
Hayʼat Tahrir al-ShamKept its collaborations with foreign intelligence secret while selectively revealing its leaders to retain legitimacy.

What counterinsurgency should do

Against inclusive insurgenciesCompete for the population: bottom-up property rights, local governance alternatives, and government transparency.
Against exclusive insurgenciesTargeted intelligence: amplify internal divisions, sow distrust between leaders and operatives, and fund exit paths for disillusioned members.
