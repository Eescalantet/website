Night Watchers and Terrorists

Why did Peru's peasant patrols govern themselves by assembly in the north and by commandos in the south?

Edwar E. Escalante · Journal of Economic Behavior and Organization 171 (2020): 116–131 · doi:10.1016/j.jebo.2019.12.025
North · vs. cattle rustlersElected presidents, a sovereign assembly, women's enforcement committees, reconciliation.

Chapter I

Peasants against terror

In 1980 the Shining Path opened its war on the Peruvian state by burning a voting center in Chuschi, Ayacucho. Over the next seventeen years it became the most violent communist insurgency in the western hemisphere. One of the most effective responses did not come from the army. It came from the peasants themselves.

The patrols, rondas campesinas, here called Night Watchers, had little money and no experience, and for years they were illegal. Thousands of indigenous communities formed them anyway. They were extralegal but not criminal: they organized outside the law, without profit motives or systematic crime.

Every patrol faced the same problem. To defend a village you have to give someone the right to use force. Patrollers can then use that right for themselves: shirk the night shift, settle personal scores, pocket the beer money. So the question the paper asks is:

How does the kind of external threat a group faces shape how it governs itself?

Chapter II

One movement, two constitutions

The first Night Watcher patrol was formed in 1976 by the community of Cuyumalca in northern Peru to fight theft and assault. The southern patrols appeared around 1983 to fight the Shining Path. Both were peasant initiatives that later entered the legal system. Inside, they looked nothing alike.

Chapter III

The shadow of the future

The theory draws on Buchanan and Tullock's Calculus of Consent (1962). Any rule for collective decisions involves two costs:

Decision-making costs: the time and effort needed to reach agreement. They rise as more people must consent.
External costs: what you expect to suffer when a decision goes against you, such as being sent on a night shift or punished on a leader's word. They rise as fewer people must consent.

A community picks the rule that minimizes the sum of the two. The paper's contribution is to show that the time horizon moves both curves at once. An existential threat with no predictable pattern shortens the horizon. Deliberation becomes slow and dangerous, so decision-making costs rise. People are also more willing to tolerate a strongman, because they expect the emergency, and the strongman, not to last.

Which mistake can you afford?

Any enforcement system makes two kinds of error. The time horizon decides which one a community would rather make.

Long horizon: risk false negativesSometimes a shirker or thief goes unpunished. That is tolerable, because villagers will keep dealing with each other for years and can correct the error later.
Short horizon: risk false positivesSometimes a cooperator is punished. That is tolerable too, because either the terrorists win or they lose, and the costs of a commando's rule may not last.

The paper's main hypothesis follows. Peasant vigilantes rely on hierarchy to enforce their agreements if and only if they face a short time horizon and an existential threat.

Chapter IV · North

Everyone knows what's going on

The northern threat was serious. Bands of cattle rustlers raided at night, killed and raped, and sold through chains of truckers, dealers and slaughterhouses. But they did not want to rule the villages, and their raids were persistent and predictable. Peasants still lived off the land, so their horizon stayed long.

A long horizon makes reputation work. A patroller who cheats loses future income and standing, so the arrangement enforces itself:

"A knowledge of 'what's going on' meant that a prosperous store-owner could not plead poverty to escape a fine for missing patrol for a month. A woman known for letting her pigs stray into neighboring fields could not claim never to have allowed the animals to roam free."Starn (1999, 139)

Peer-to-peer enforcement. Women's committees made sure the men patrolled. A man could fight back against other men, but attacking a group of señoras would make him a laughingstock:

"Those who dodged service could expect to be roused out of bed by the wives of other villagers, fined, or even whipped."Starn (1999, 78)

Checks on the patrol itself. The assembly elected and removed leaders and banned patrols from acting for private gain. When a whipping was ordered, a relative often held the lash, and anyone who held back was whipped too. Justice aimed at reconciliation, because everyone had to keep living together:

"We're all peasants, all poor. Why punish someone so bad they can't work? Or send them to rot in jail? How will their families survive? In the night watch you take your whip strokes, and then correct yourself, and go back to your life."Villager quoted in Starn (1999, 88)

The checks worked almost too well. Leading a patrol was so draining and closely watched that volunteers were hard to find. "This job tires you out. The problems never end, and people are never satisfied, and talk about you behind your back."

Chapter V · South

A thousand eyes, a thousand ears

The Shining Path was a different kind of enemy. It wanted to replace the community's order. It killed village authorities, carried out random public executions, and planted informers inside the villages. In the words it used for itself, it had "one thousand ears and one thousand eyes." Forming a patrol made a village a target:

"Against the use of the military and their peasant supporters, we responded with a forceful action: Lucanamarca. … We executed 80 villagers."Abimael Guzmán, quoted in TRC (2003)

With no predictable pattern to plan around, deliberation became slow and dangerous, and no one knew which neighbor was a spy. So communities handed authority to commandos. Villagers had to register and carry passes. Passwords, flag ceremonies and training became compulsory. Commerce was restricted. Planning took place at night in the hills, in secret:

"No one could know, it was a matter of life and death; we were forbidden from even telling our wives because people said there were gossips."Patrol leader, quoted in La Serna (2012, 170)

Punishment in the south looked more like the terrorists' own methods than like northern reconciliation. Falling asleep on guard could mean execution. The hierarchy also brought its own internal rent-seeking, which Del Pino (1992) documents: commandos settled personal disputes, seized resources, and abused outsiders.

"They were like gods… they acted like kings, and with the positions they held, they practically were kings."Peasant from the Apurímac valley, in La Serna (2012, 203)

Villagers accepted these external costs because they expected them to be temporary. Like other revolutionary movements, the threat was not expected to last.

Chapter VI

The experiment in reverse

If the time horizon drives structure, hierarchy should weaken when terror recedes. Coronel's (1996) fieldwork in Huanta, Ayacucho, lets us follow three southern communities through the conflict. In all three, authority went to the commandos as violence rose and returned to the assembly as violence fell.

Chapter VII

Ruling out the usual suspects

Three alternative explanations are plausible. The evidence rejects each one. Click a hypothesis to see why.

H1Long horizons and non-existential threats produce horizontal structures; short horizons and existential threats produce vertical ones.Supported
Northern patrols facing predictable crime governed by assembly. Southern patrols facing random, existential terror centralized under commandos. Southern communities moved back toward assemblies once terror declined.
H2Hierarchy arises whenever villagers must make snap decisions.Rejected
Northern patrols also made split-second choices, such as chasing thieves and answering night raids, yet they stayed horizontal. The need for fast combat decisions alone does not produce hierarchy. The time horizon does.
H3The army imposed the vertical structure from outside.Rejected
Southern peasants organized from about 1983, roughly a decade before the armed forces formally allied with them. Patrols grew from fewer than 700 in 1989 to more than 1,200 by 1991, and they kept their autonomy after the alliance, choosing their own presidents, treasurers and commandos. Legislative Decree 741 (1991) recognized patrols that already existed. In Sanamarca: "The army did not impose the patrols; we have chosen our leaders, and we all agree to fight."
H4Different cultures explain the different structures.Rejected
Neither region had self-defense organizations before the crime wave and the Shining Path. Traditional southern authorities, the varayoqs, clashed with both the Shining Path and the army, and later shared power with commandos rather than producing them. As Starn writes of the north, the ties of community "were by no means a received tradition in northern villages. They had to be created."
Chapter VIII

The lesson

The internal structure of extralegal organizations is especially sensitive to the shadow of the future.

Hierarchy and democracy were not cultural inheritances or orders from the state. They were tools. Peasants chose whichever one kept the cost of controlling opportunism lowest given how far ahead they could plan. When tomorrow was predictable, they governed in a circle where "it was easy for anyone to get in a word." When it was not, they accepted kings, and they took the kings back down once the threat had passed.

The Night Watchers grew into a force of about 120,000 combatants. Fighting alongside some 30,000 soldiers, they helped defeat the Shining Path. The logic of the paper, that threats shape governance through the time horizon, applies to self-defense groups, militias and community policing wherever the state is absent.
