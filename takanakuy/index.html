Fighting on Christmas

In the Peruvian highlands, neighbors settle the year's grudges with a public fistfight, then embrace and feast together. Why does it work?

Edwar E. Escalante and Raymond J. March · "Fighting on Christmas: Brawling as Self-Governance in Rural Peru," Journal of Institutional Economics · doi:10.1017/S1744137419000468
Chapter I

"To hit each other"

Every Christmas morning, people from across Chumbivilcas Province in southern Peru fill the municipal coliseum of Santo Tomás, where bullfights are held the rest of the year. They come to watch neighbors fight. The ritual is called Takanakuy, Quechua for "to hit each other."

People fight over land boundaries, stolen cattle, broken agreements, romantic betrayals, schoolyard quarrels and insults traded while drunk at a fiesta. Every fight begins and ends with an embrace. Then the whole village eats together.

A fistfight looks like a strange way to make peace. The paper argues it is a sensible one. Where formal courts are distant, slow and distrusted, Takanakuy gives villagers a credible way to enforce the law. It ends feuds before they spread, and it turns a potential social cost into a public spectacle that the community enjoys.

"Any friction … finds a solution this day … through fist fighting and under fair conditions. This way, with no hypocrisy, fighters eliminate tensions and initiate the next year free from resentment."Laime (2003, 116)

Chapter III

Rules of the ring

Takanakuy is not a free-for-all. Volunteer families called karguyoc organize it: they arrange a Mass, raise funds and prepare the feast. Referees, usually local authorities whom villagers consider impartial, police every fight. They also lose reputation if they let a one-sided fight get out of hand. How well do you know the rules?

The rules keep the fights from becoming dangerous, though not perfectly. Of 134 brawlers at Santo Tomás in 2006, Trelles (2010) found that 9 were severely injured, and one of them died. The rules also contain the dispute itself. The fight happens once, in public, under supervision, and then it is over.

"Since the day we fought, now he respects me, now we greet each other with no problems, now he is good people, and we go along peacefully."A fighter, quoted in Laime (2003, 61)
Chapter IV

"Apologize, or I will fight you at the Takanakuy"

Without a state to enforce the rules, the victim's only threat is private force. That threat is weak when using it is dangerous. If a victim kills an offender, the offender's kin take revenge on the killer. Takanakuy changes the calculation for both sides.

What happens

Reputation makes the threat credible

Backing down is costly. A villager who apologizes when he believes he is right, or who fails to answer a wrong, is marked as a wimp and becomes an easy target. Fighting earns status, even in defeat. Weaker fighters who face a stronger opponent gain a reputation for being fearless. The champions become qorilazos, the skilled and tough ranch hands of local legend, a status that can "level and even invert the ethnic and class hierarchies" of the province.

Families and friends join the fight too, taking on later bouts on a fighter's behalf. That works as a deterrent. Someone everybody sees as being in the wrong will struggle to find allies willing to fight for him, and may be challenged more than once.

"When a fighter wins a battle, the victory extends to all his/her family. In the case of the ayllu, the winner fighter becomes the favorite son/daughter of the community and the chosen one to hold a local position since it is believed that he/she will be able to defend the ayllu and to maintain order."Tello (2018, 68)
Chapter V

Three hypotheses

H1Villagers rely on Takanakuy when formal ways of resolving conflict are unavailable or insufficient.Supported
Reaching a court in Cusco or Arequipa means several buses and trucks that "make the trip, at best, once a week," followed by long waits for a judge. Peruvian women have faced a long history of discrimination in the courts, and children under 16 need a legal representative. Corruption is common: "sometimes authorities are corrupt, they may receive a couple of cows or lamps. The cattle rustler corrupts authorities that way. Then, what can the affected party do? He has to look for revenge." Takanakuy is open to anyone who agrees to fight and shows up, women and children included.
H2Takanakuy persists because it ends feuds, prevents violence outside the ritual and removes obstacles to cooperation.Supported
Unresolved grudges are costly in small communities that farm and manage resources together. Ostracism is not a practical punishment when the village needs every pair of hands. Without the ritual's rules, fights can become a waykilla, a general brawl of all against all, which "has resulted in deaths in previous years as a consequence of the stones thrown in different directions." As Tello puts it, the ritual achieves "the social containment of vengeance," and "the peace that emerges from the festival is long-lasting, and it is what makes life in community possible."
H3The reputational gains from Takanakuy make a wronged villager likely to carry out the threat to fight, despite the risks.Supported
Fighters and their families gain social standing, and good fighters often go on to lead the community's patrols against cattle rustlers and to represent peasant organizations. Even outmatched fighters gain by being seen as fearless. Because the fights are public, on a major holiday and in front of hundreds of people, information about who keeps their word spreads quickly.
Chapter VI

What happens when you ban it

Outsiders have tried to stop Takanakuy several times. Each attempt met hostility, and none succeeded.

  • The policeBanned the ritual on one occasion. The fighting moved into the streets, unsupervised, and fights, injuries and deaths all increased (Laime 2003).
  • The Catholic ChurchOrganized competing events during the festival and threatened organizers, participants and spectators with excommunication. One priest called it "insulting God on his birthday, and the devil's work."
  • State television, 2014Promoted a documentary on the festival, then pulled it and condemned children's participation. The filmmaker and researchers note that adults make sure children do not hurt themselves.

In contrast, the provincial government advertises the tradition and acknowledges that its purpose is conflict resolution.

Chapter VII

The lesson

Where courts are out of reach, a well-governed fight can be cheaper, fairer and more final than the alternatives.

Brawling costs more than a working court system or an immediate mutual apology. The risk of injury is real. But for villagers who have neither, the choice is between regulated violence and unregulated revenge. Takanakuy settles many conflicts through a single structure, restores relationships immediately, and spares villagers from searching for impartial judges or building their own enforcement. Its rules, its audience and the reputations at stake turn a dangerous threat into a credible and contained one.

The paper adds to the economics of informal governance, which includes pirate constitutions, prison gangs, trial by battle and Somali customary law. It also extends the economics of dueling by looking at what a fighting ritual does for the whole community, not only for the fighters.
